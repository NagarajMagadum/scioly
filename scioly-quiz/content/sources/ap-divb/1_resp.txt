## F. Short Answer and Case Studies

? [4] Name the three ways CO2 is carried in blood, with approximate percentages. Explain the roles of carbonic anhydrase and the chloride shift.
= ~70% as bicarbonate, ~20–23% as carbaminohemoglobin, ~7–10% dissolved. Carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells rapidly converts CO2 + H2O to H2CO3, which splits into H+ and HCO3−. HCO3− leaves the cell in exchange for Cl− (chloride shift), keeping the gradient so more CO2 can load.

? [2] CO2 is a neutral molecule. Explain, with an equation, how rising CO2 lowers blood pH.
= CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. More CO2 drives the reaction right, raising H+ and lowering pH (respiratory acidosis).

? [3] A person lacks carbonic anhydrase. Predict what happens to CO2 transport and blood pH.
= CO2 and carbonic acid cannot interconvert quickly, so far less CO2 is carried as bicarbonate. CO2 builds up in tissues and blood, lowering pH (acidosis).

? [5] Explain why hemoglobin's sigmoidal curve is useful. Include positive cooperativity and what happens at about 40 mmHg.
= Binding of the first O2 changes hemoglobin's shape and raises affinity for the next O2 (positive cooperativity). In the lungs (~100 mmHg) the flat top keeps hemoglobin nearly 100% saturated even if PO2 drops a little. At tissue PO2 (~40 mmHg) the curve is steep, so a small drop in PO2 releases a lot of O2.

? [5] Why does fetal hemoglobin's curve lie to the left of adult hemoglobin's? Why is this useful? What disease can be treated by inducing HbF?
= HbF has 2 alpha and 2 gamma chains; gamma chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so HbF has higher O2 affinity. This pulls O2 from maternal blood across the placenta. Sickle cell disease: hydroxyurea raises HbF, which prevents sickling.

? [2] How does the myoglobin dissociation curve differ in shape from hemoglobin's, and why?
= Myoglobin's curve is hyperbolic and left-shifted because it has one heme and no cooperative binding. It stores O2 in muscle and releases it only at very low PO2.

? [3] Define surfactant generally. Explain why pulmonary surfactant is important, using the law of Laplace (P = 2T/r).
= A surfactant lowers the surface tension of water. Without it, surface tension would make small alveoli (small r, high pressure) collapse and empty into larger ones. Surfactant lowers tension more in small alveoli, keeping all alveoli open and reducing the work of breathing.

? [4] Donald coughs up thick mucus and has high chloride in his sweat. Name the disease, how it is inherited and the affected protein. Explain why his mucus is thick and why the pH of his small intestine is lower than normal.
= Cystic fibrosis; autosomal recessive; CFTR chloride channel. Without Cl− secretion, Na+ and water are pulled out of the airway, leaving dehydrated, sticky mucus. Pancreatic duct cells need CFTR to secrete bicarbonate, so less HCO3− reaches the duodenum and its pH drops; thick secretions also block the ducts.

? [3] A boy born in 2000 fails to gain weight, has greasy, foul-smelling stools and a productive cough by 4 months. Name the disease, the most common mutation, and one bacterium he is especially prone to.
= Cystic fibrosis; ΔF508 in CFTR; Pseudomonas aeruginosa (also Staphylococcus aureus).

? [3] How is cystic fibrosis diagnosed and treated? Is there a cure?
= Newborn screening (high immunoreactive trypsinogen), sweat chloride test, genetic testing. Treatment: airway clearance, mucolytics, antibiotics, bronchodilators, pancreatic enzymes, CFTR modulators. No cure; lung transplant in severe cases.

? [4] A stab wound causes a collapsed left lung. Using Palv, Pip and Ptp, explain why it collapsed. Why did the right lung not collapse? Name two risk factors for a spontaneous pneumothorax.
= Air enters the pleural space, so Pip rises to atmospheric pressure. Ptp (Palv − Pip) falls to 0, so nothing holds the lung open and elastic recoil collapses it. Each lung has its own pleural cavity. Risk factors: tall, thin young male; smoking; COPD or CF; Marfan syndrome.

? [4] Left heart failure causes blood to back up in the pulmonary circulation. Explain how this causes pulmonary edema. Give two reasons PO2 falls more than PCO2 rises when diffusion is impaired.
= Higher pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid into the interstitium and alveoli, thickening the diffusion barrier. (1) CO2 is about 20× more soluble and diffuses faster. (2) Extra ventilation easily blows off CO2 but cannot add much O2 to blood leaving well-ventilated alveoli (hemoglobin is already near saturation).

? [5] Jeffrey, an obese heavy smoker with heart disease, suddenly becomes short of breath, wheezes and coughs up pink, frothy sputum. Name the condition and whether it is acute or chronic. Give two immediate treatments and two tests.
= Acute pulmonary edema (cardiogenic). Treatment: sit upright, oxygen, diuretic (furosemide), nitrates/positive-pressure ventilation. Tests: chest x-ray, echocardiogram, BNP blood test, ECG, arterial blood gas.

? [3] Players collapsed during a game at 7,400 ft. Explain the effect of altitude on O2 loading and unloading, and name two ways the body adapts over weeks.
= Lower atmospheric PO2 lowers alveolar PO2, so less O2 loads onto hemoglobin and delivery to muscles falls. Adaptations: faster/deeper breathing, more EPO and red cells, more 2,3-BPG, more capillaries and mitochondria.

? [4] Climbers new to high altitude breathe much faster. Will this cause acidosis or alkalosis? Explain using the bicarbonate equation. What are the two dangerous forms of altitude sickness?
= Respiratory alkalosis: rapid breathing removes CO2, the equation shifts left, H+ falls and pH rises. Dangerous forms: high-altitude pulmonary edema and high-altitude cerebral edema.

? [3] After scuba diving, Jeff boards a plane and develops elbow pain. What is happening, which gas law explains it, and what is the treatment?
= Decompression sickness: extra N2 dissolved in blood under pressure forms bubbles as pressure drops. Henry's law. Treat with hyperbaric oxygen (recompression).

? [5] Randy, a pack-a-day smoker, has worsening shortness of breath. His FEV1/FVC is 52% and his DLCO is low. Name the disease and give the reasoning. Is his residual volume increased or decreased? Name two things you would do to help him.
= Emphysema: obstructive pattern (ratio < 70%) plus low DLCO from destroyed alveolar surface (chronic bronchitis usually has normal DLCO). RV is increased (air trapping). Help: stop smoking, bronchodilators/inhaled steroids, pulmonary rehab, oxygen, vaccines.

? [4] Emphysema: name the main symptom, explain what causes it physiologically, and describe what happens over time.
= Main symptom: shortness of breath. Destroyed alveolar walls reduce gas-exchange area and elastic recoil, so air is trapped and exhaling is hard. Over time, alveoli merge into large air spaces (bullae), the chest becomes barrel-shaped and breathing work rises.

? [3] Overstimulated pneumotaxic center: describe the rate and depth of breathing, whether hypercapnia or hypocapnia results, and whether gas exchange becomes more efficient.
= Rapid, shallow breathing; hypocapnia; no, a larger share of each small breath is dead space, so alveolar ventilation is less efficient.

? [2] Why are the C-rings of the trachea incomplete posteriorly?
= So the esophagus behind the trachea can bulge forward when a large bolus is swallowed; the trachealis muscle also changes airway diameter for coughing.

? [3] Describe what happens during swallowing to keep food out of the airway.
= The soft palate and uvula rise to close off the nasopharynx; the larynx rises and the epiglottis folds over the glottis; the vocal cords close; breathing briefly stops.

? [3] What force must be overcome during inspiration? Why does the lung not collapse at rest?
= The inward elastic recoil of the lungs (and alveolar surface tension). The negative intrapleural pressure and cohesion of pleural fluid hold the lung against the chest wall, and surfactant reduces surface tension.

? [2] What happens to PO2 and PCO2 during voluntary hyperventilation?
= Alveolar PO2 rises (toward ~120 mmHg) and PCO2 falls (toward ~20 mmHg).

? [3] A 55-year-old obese man snores loudly, has morning headaches and is sleepy during the day. Name the likely disorder, its mechanism, how it is diagnosed and treated.
= Obstructive sleep apnea: repeated collapse of the pharyngeal airway during sleep. Diagnosed by polysomnogram; treated with CPAP, weight loss, oral appliances or surgery.

? [3 | TB] In the Weibel airway model, how does the partial pressure of H2O, CO2 and O2 in generation 1 (bronchi) compare with generation 22 (alveolar sacs) after inhalation?
= H2O: less in generation 1 (accept "same"; air is nearly fully humidified by the trachea); CO2: less in generation 1; O2: greater in generation 1. Fresh air mixes with alveolar gas, so CO2 rises and O2 falls toward the alveoli.

? [2] How would a pulmonary embolism and a right-to-left shunt each affect alveolar dead space?
= Pulmonary embolism increases alveolar dead space (ventilated, not perfused). A shunt does not increase dead space; it is perfused but not ventilated (V/Q = 0).


? [5] Compare the effects of a single bout of exercise with long-term training on the respiratory system.
= Single bout: ventilation rises sharply (deeper and faster breaths) from neural input then chemical fine-tuning; more O2 unloaded (curve shifts right with heat, CO2 and H+); more lung capillaries open, so perfusion and diffusion rise. Training: stronger breathing muscles and higher maximum ventilation; more capillaries and mitochondria in muscle, so muscles extract more O2; lower breathing rate at a given workload; lung volumes and alveoli number change little.

? [4] Compare chronic bronchitis and emphysema: what is damaged, the main symptom, typical blood gases and appearance.
= Chronic bronchitis: inflamed airways, enlarged mucous glands, damaged cilia; productive cough; early hypoxemia and CO2 retention, cyanosis and edema ("blue bloater"). Emphysema: destroyed alveolar walls and lost elastic recoil; shortness of breath; breathes hard to keep gases near normal, thin with barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing ("pink puffer").

? [4] Explain why an asthma attack makes it harder to exhale than to inhale.
= During inhalation, the expanding lungs pull airways open. During exhalation, the pressure around the airways rises and squeezes already narrowed, inflamed, mucus-filled bronchioles shut, trapping air (wheezing on exhalation).

? [3] Name three common asthma triggers and two types of asthma medicines (with how each works).
= Triggers: allergens (pollen, dust mites, pet dander), exercise, cold air, smoke, infections, NSAIDs. Medicines: quick-relief beta-2 agonists (albuterol) relax bronchial smooth muscle; inhaled corticosteroids reduce inflammation; leukotriene blockers (montelukast) block inflammatory mediators.

? [4] A 72-year-old has fever, a cough producing rusty sputum, chest pain on breathing and crackles over one lung. Name the likely diagnosis and organism. Explain why his blood O2 is low.
= Lobar pneumonia, most likely Streptococcus pneumoniae. Alveoli fill with fluid, pus and cells, so blood flowing past them is not oxygenated (shunt), lowering PaO2.

? [3] Why does obstructive sleep apnea raise the risk of high blood pressure and heart disease?
= Repeated drops in blood O2 and arousals trigger sympathetic surges (high heart rate and blood pressure) many times a night; long-term this raises blood pressure and strains the heart, increasing risk of heart failure, arrhythmia and stroke.

? [5] Describe cystic fibrosis at each level: gene, protein, cell, organ and whole person.
= Gene: mutations in CFTR (chromosome 7), autosomal recessive (ΔF508 most common). Protein: misfolded or nonfunctional chloride channel. Cell: epithelial cells cannot secrete Cl−, so water does not follow and mucus is thick and dehydrated. Organs: lungs (blocked airways, chronic Pseudomonas infections), pancreas (blocked ducts, malabsorption, CF-related diabetes), intestines (meconium ileus), sweat glands (salty sweat), vas deferens. Whole person: chronic cough, poor growth, shortened life expectancy, treated with airway clearance, enzymes, antibiotics and CFTR modulators.
