## F. Short Answer and Case Studies

? [3] Give three differences between innate and adaptive immunity.
= Innate: present from birth, nonspecific, acts in minutes to hours, no memory, germline-encoded receptors (PRRs). Adaptive: acquired, specific to one antigen, takes days the first time, has memory, receptors made by gene rearrangement.

? [4] Name the cardinal signs of inflammation and explain how histamine produces two of them.
= Redness, heat, swelling, pain (and loss of function). Histamine dilates arterioles (redness, heat) and makes capillaries leaky, so fluid enters tissue (swelling) and presses on nerves (pain).

? [2] Give one reason a high fever helps and one reason it can harm.
= Helps: slows microbial growth, the liver and spleen hold back iron and zinc, speeds repair and immune activity. Harms: very high fever denatures proteins, can cause seizures and dehydration, and raises heart rate and O2 demand.

? [5] Name five ways antibodies inactivate antigens.
= Neutralization, agglutination, precipitation, complement activation (fixation), opsonization (also antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity).

? [8] Complete the table of hypersensitivity types.
| Type | Mediator | Example |
| I | | |
| II | | |
| III | | |
| IV | | |
= I (immediate): IgE on mast cells and basophils, histamine — anaphylaxis, hay fever, asthma. II (cytotoxic): IgG/IgM against cell-surface antigens, complement — transfusion reaction, HDN, Graves', myasthenia gravis. III (immune complex): antigen–antibody complexes deposited in tissue — serum sickness, lupus, Arthus reaction. IV (delayed): T cells and macrophages — contact dermatitis, TB skin test, transplant rejection.

? [2] Why can a bone marrow transplant from an identical twin be given with little risk of rejection?
= An identical twin has the same MHC (HLA) proteins, so the cells are recognized as self; there is little rejection or graft-versus-host disease.

? [3] Cancer cells are "self." How can the immune system still recognize them? Use the terms MHC I and neoantigen, and name two cells that kill them.
= Mutated proteins are broken down and their fragments (neoantigens) are shown on MHC I, where cytotoxic T cells recognize them as nonself. Cancer cells that hide by lowering MHC I are killed by NK cells. Cells: CD8 cytotoxic T cells and NK cells.

? [4 | TB] Describe T-cell development in the thymus, including positive and negative selection.
= Precursors from bone marrow enter the thymus and build T-cell receptors (double negative → double positive CD4+CD8+). Positive selection (cortex): cells that bind self-MHC weakly survive; those that cannot bind die. Cells become CD4 (MHC II) or CD8 (MHC I) single positive. Negative selection (medulla): cells that bind self-antigen too strongly undergo apoptosis, creating self-tolerance. Survivors leave as naive T cells.

? [2] How does a B cell switch from making IgM to IgG or IgE?
= Class switching: helper T cells give signals (CD40L and cytokines such as IL-4 for IgE) that make the B cell recombine its DNA, swapping the heavy-chain constant region while keeping the same variable region and specificity.

? [4] Name the four kinds of acquired immunity with one example each.
= Natural active – recovering from chickenpox. Artificial active – a vaccine. Natural passive – maternal IgG across the placenta or IgA in breast milk. Artificial passive – antivenom or IVIG.

? [3] Describe the complement cascade and its three main outcomes.
= Complement proteins circulate inactive and are activated in a cascade by the classical (antibody), lectin or alternative (microbial surface) pathway, all converging on cleavage of C3. Outcomes: opsonization (C3b), inflammation and chemotaxis (C3a, C5a), and lysis by the membrane attack complex (C5b–C9).

? [3] Why do complement proteins circulate in an inactive (pro-) form?
= They are powerful inducers of inflammation and cell lysis; keeping them inactive until a pathogen is detected protects the body's own cells.

? [2] How does the innate immune system recognize pathogens?
= Pattern recognition receptors (such as toll-like receptors) bind pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) shared by broad classes of microbes, such as LPS, flagellin and viral double-stranded RNA.

? [5] AIDS: what type of pathogen causes it? Describe its effect on weight, lymph nodes and body temperature. Can people with HIV live long lives today?
= A retrovirus (HIV). Weight loss (wasting), swollen lymph nodes and recurring fevers. Yes — antiretroviral therapy suppresses the virus so people can live near-normal lifespans.

? [4] Explain how HIV replicates and give two drug targets.
= HIV gp120 binds CD4 and a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4); gp41 fuses the membranes; reverse transcriptase copies RNA into DNA; integrase inserts it into host DNA; new proteins are cut by protease and virions bud off. Drug targets: entry/fusion, reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease.

? [4] A 45-year-old woman has constipation, fatigue, weight gain, dry skin and a lump in her neck. Name the disease, the antibodies elevated, the hormone results and one treatment.
= Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Anti-thyroid peroxidase and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies. High TSH, low T3 and T4. Levothyroxine.

? [5] Clara, 33, has had mononucleosis and now has dizziness, arm weakness and tingling, and partial loss of vision in one eye. MRI shows white-matter lesions. Name the disease and give four supporting reasons. How is it treated? Can it be cured?
= Multiple sclerosis: neurological symptoms in different places at different times; optic neuritis (vision loss); MRI lesions in CNS white matter (demyelination); young woman; prior Epstein-Barr virus (mono) is a known risk factor; oligoclonal bands in cerebrospinal fluid. Treatment: corticosteroids for flares, disease-modifying drugs (interferon beta, monoclonal antibodies), physical therapy. No cure.

? [5] Jonny, a pianist, has stiff, painful, red, warm, swollen finger and wrist joints, a high sedimentation rate and abnormal antibodies. Name the disease. What is happening in the joints? Would neutrophils in his joint fluid be normal, high or low? Can he be cured? Name one long-term treatment.
= Rheumatoid arthritis. The immune system attacks the synovial membrane, which thickens (pannus) and erodes cartilage and bone. High — neutrophils flood inflamed joints. No cure. DMARDs (e.g., methotrexate), biologics (TNF inhibitors), NSAIDs, low-dose steroids, physical therapy.

? [4] A boy has a red facial rash, fever, painful joints, hair loss and swollen lymph nodes. Name the disease and give two treatments. Should he keep playing soccer outdoors?
= Systemic lupus erythematosus. Hydroxychloroquine, NSAIDs, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants. He can stay active (exercise helps) but must use strong sun protection and rest during flares.

? [5] A patient has an anaphylactic reaction. Explain what happens in the body, the blood test that confirms it, and how epinephrine helps.
= Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils, which release histamine and other mediators: vasodilation drops blood pressure, leaky vessels cause swelling, bronchi constrict. Serum tryptase confirms mast-cell activation. Epinephrine constricts blood vessels (raises pressure) and relaxes airway smooth muscle.

? [2] What is the difference between a food allergy and a food intolerance?
= An allergy is an immune (often IgE) response to a food protein. An intolerance does not involve the immune system, such as lactose intolerance caused by missing lactase.

? [6] Transplant patients: Patient 3's graft clots within minutes; Patient 4 has reduced kidney function and flu-like symptoms 2 months after transplant; Patient 5's liver loses bile ducts over years. What condition do they share, why does it occur, and what is each type called?
= Transplant rejection: the recipient's immune system recognizes donor HLA as foreign. Patient 3: hyperacute (pre-existing antibodies; remove graft). Patient 4: acute (T-cell mediated; treat with higher-dose immunosuppressants and steroids). Patient 5: chronic rejection.

? [2] Why does an Rh-negative mother usually not harm her first Rh-positive baby?
= She is only sensitized at delivery when fetal blood enters her circulation. Her first antibodies are IgM, which cannot cross the placenta; IgG made in later pregnancies does cross it.

? [3] Explain why someone who had chickenpox as a child usually does not get it again.
= Memory B and T cells made in the first infection respond quickly and strongly on re-exposure (secondary response), producing high levels of IgG before the virus can cause disease.

? [3] Why does a person with HIV become susceptible to infections that would not harm a healthy person?
= HIV destroys CD4 helper T cells, which activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages. With few helper cells, both antibody and cell-mediated responses fail, so opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis pneumonia, TB, candidiasis, Kaposi sarcoma) take hold.

? [2] Explain how the thymus changes with age and why.
= It is large in children and shrinks after puberty, with lymphoid tissue replaced by fat (thymic involution), as part of immune aging and influenced by sex hormones and steroids. Fewer new T cells are made, but existing memory T cells persist.


? [5] Describe the sequence of events in an allergic reaction from first exposure to symptoms.
= Sensitization: an allergen is taken up by APCs and shown to Th2 cells; IL-4 drives B cells to make IgE, which binds Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils. Re-exposure: the allergen cross-links bound IgE, triggering degranulation. Histamine, leukotrienes and prostaglandins cause vasodilation, leaky vessels, smooth-muscle contraction, mucus and itching within minutes. A late phase hours later brings eosinophils and more inflammation.

? [5] Describe the primary immune response to a new bacterial infection, from entry to antibody production.
= Barriers are breached; complement and neutrophils/macrophages respond (inflammation). Dendritic cells capture antigen, travel to a lymph node and present it on MHC II to helper T cells. Helper T cells activate B cells specific to the antigen (CD40L, cytokines). B cells proliferate (clonal selection), class-switch and become plasma cells making antibodies (IgM first, then IgG) and memory cells. Antibodies neutralize, opsonize and activate complement. Peak about 10–14 days.

? [4] Compare humoral and cell-mediated immunity.
= Humoral: B cells and antibodies in body fluids; targets extracellular pathogens and toxins; antigens recognized directly. Cell-mediated: T cells; CD8 cells kill infected or cancer cells, CD4 cells direct the response; antigen recognized only on MHC; key in viral infections, cancer and transplant rejection.

? [4] Describe the four steps of acute inflammation and its purpose.
= Injury or infection triggers mast cells and macrophages to release histamine, cytokines and other mediators. Vasodilation and increased permeability bring fluid, proteins and clotting factors (redness, heat, swelling, pain). Phagocytes are mobilized (margination, diapedesis, chemotaxis) and destroy microbes. Repair follows. Purpose: contain and destroy pathogens, remove debris and begin healing.

? [4] Explain how toll-like receptors link innate and adaptive immunity.
= TLRs on dendritic cells and macrophages detect PAMPs, activating NF-κB and the release of cytokines. This makes dendritic cells mature, display more MHC and co-stimulatory molecules (B7) and travel to lymph nodes, where they activate naive T cells, starting the adaptive response.

? [5] A 30-year-old has had repeated sinus infections, pneumonia and diarrhea for years. Tests show low IgG and IgA, normal B-cell numbers and a poor response to the tetanus vaccine. Name the disorder, explain the defect, and give its treatment.
= Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID). B cells are present but fail to mature into plasma cells and make antibodies, so the person cannot clear encapsulated bacteria. Treatment: lifelong IVIG or subcutaneous immunoglobulin, and antibiotics for infections.

? [5] A 4-month-old boy has thrush, chronic diarrhea, failure to thrive and Pneumocystis pneumonia. He has very few T cells. Name the disorder, explain why both T and B immunity fail, and give two treatments.
= Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID). Without functional T cells, B cells cannot be activated, so both cell-mediated and antibody immunity fail. Treatment: bone marrow (stem cell) transplant, gene therapy, enzyme replacement (for ADA-SCID), IVIG; avoid live vaccines.

? [5] Describe the stages of HIV infection and how it is diagnosed.
= Acute infection (2–4 weeks): flu-like illness, high viral load. Clinical latency: few symptoms for years while CD4 counts fall slowly. AIDS: CD4 below 200 cells/µL or an AIDS-defining illness (Pneumocystis pneumonia, Kaposi sarcoma, TB). Diagnosed with antigen/antibody tests (p24 antigen and anti-HIV antibodies), confirmed with viral load testing.

? [5] Compare rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis: target, cells involved, symptoms and treatment.
= RA: synovial joint lining; T cells, B cells (rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP), macrophages; symmetric painful, swollen joints with morning stiffness; DMARDs (methotrexate), biologics (TNF inhibitors), steroids, NSAIDs. MS: CNS myelin; autoreactive T cells, B cells, macrophages; vision loss, numbness, weakness, balance problems in flares; steroids for flares, disease-modifying drugs (interferon beta, anti-CD20 antibodies).

? [4] Compare Graves' disease and Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
= Graves': antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor; hyperthyroid (weight loss, heat intolerance, fast heart rate, bulging eyes, goiter); low TSH, high T3/T4; treated with antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine or surgery. Hashimoto's: T cells and anti-TPO/thyroglobulin antibodies destroy the gland; hypothyroid (weight gain, fatigue, cold intolerance, dry skin); high TSH, low T4; treated with levothyroxine.

? [4] A teacher has drooping eyelids and double vision by the end of each school day, and her arm tires when writing on the board. Name the disorder and explain the mechanism. What test and treatment are used?
= Myasthenia gravis: antibodies block and destroy acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction (type II), so repeated stimulation weakens contraction. Test: anti-AChR antibodies (also the ice-pack or edrophonium test, EMG). Treatment: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine), immunosuppressants, thymectomy, IVIG or plasmapheresis for crises.

? [4] A child gets a tetanus booster and 8 hours later has a painful, red, swollen area at the injection site. A week after receiving a drug made from animal serum, another patient develops fever, rash and joint pain. Name each reaction, its type and its mechanism.
= First: Arthus reaction (type III, local) — high antibody levels form immune complexes with the antigen in local vessel walls, activating complement and neutrophils. Second: serum sickness (type III, systemic) — antibodies to foreign proteins form complexes that deposit in joints, skin and kidneys.

? [3] A gardener develops an itchy, blistering rash two days after handling poison ivy. Name the condition, its hypersensitivity type and why it takes days to appear.
= Allergic contact dermatitis, type IV (delayed). Urushiol acts as a hapten; sensitized T cells must be recruited to the skin and release cytokines that attract macrophages, which takes 24–72 hours.

? [3] What is the difference between urticaria and anaphylaxis?
= Urticaria is a local skin reaction (itchy hives) from mast-cell histamine. Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic type I reaction involving two or more body systems (skin, airway, circulation, gut), with airway swelling and low blood pressure; it can kill within minutes and needs epinephrine.
