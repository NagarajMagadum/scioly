= Amylase works near neutral pH and pepsin near pH 2. If all enzymes were tested under the same conditions, both could not be active at once.

? [2] Someone eats 50 g of fat in a 2,000-kcal day. How many calories came from fat, and what percent is that?
= 50 × 9 = 450 kcal; 450 ÷ 2,000 = 22.5%

? [2] A meal contains 60 g carbohydrate, 25 g protein and 20 g fat. Calculate total calories.
= 60 × 4 + 25 × 4 + 20 × 9 = 240 + 100 + 180 = 520 kcal

? [7] Gastric emptying: normal half-emptying time is < 20 min for liquids and < 120 min for solids. A patient's times are 15 min (liquid) and 180 min (solid). True or false: a) the patient is at higher risk of malnutrition  b) the patient may have more acid reflux  c) the patient has high pyloric resistance  d) more pyloric resistance speeds solid emptying  e) stronger antral contractions without more pyloric resistance speed emptying of both  f) if the patient vomits, the vomit will contain bile  g) antacids would not help at all.
= a) T  b) T  c) T  d) F  e) T  f) F  g) F (antacids can ease reflux symptoms)

? [3] True or false: a) a tumor blocking the common bile duct lowers blood conjugated bilirubin  b) a mutation that reduces UGT activity raises unconjugated bilirubin  c) malaria, which bursts red blood cells, raises blood bilirubin.
= a) F — it raises conjugated bilirubin (it backs up into blood).  b) T  c) T

? [3] Describe carbohydrate digestion and absorption in the small intestine. Name the transporters involved.
= Pancreatic amylase breaks starch into maltose, maltotriose and dextrins; brush-border enzymes (maltase, sucrase, lactase, dextrinase) make monosaccharides. Glucose and galactose enter by SGLT1 (Na+ cotransport); fructose enters by GLUT5; all leave the cell into blood by GLUT2.

## F. Short Answer and Case Studies

? [2] Compare peristalsis and segmentation.
= Peristalsis: waves of contraction and relaxation of adjacent segments that push food forward. Segmentation: nonadjacent rings of muscle contract alternately, mixing chyme back and forth (mainly small intestine).

? [4] Where is bile made and stored, and what does it do? What pigment in bile makes skin yellow in liver disease, and where does it come from?
= Made by hepatocytes; stored and concentrated in the gallbladder. Bile salts emulsify fat and form micelles so lipase can work and fats can be absorbed; bile also excretes cholesterol and bilirubin. Bilirubin, from heme in old red blood cells, causes jaundice.

? [4] Explain how pepsin digests protein without digesting the stomach itself.
= It is secreted as inactive pepsinogen and activated only in the lumen by HCl. A thick layer of alkaline mucus from mucous cells, tight junctions between epithelial cells and rapid cell renewal (every 3–5 days) protect the wall; prostaglandins maintain the mucus barrier.

? [5] What is a retroperitoneal organ? List three retroperitoneal digestive organs.
= An organ that lies behind the parietal peritoneum, against the posterior abdominal wall, without a mesentery. Examples: most of the duodenum, pancreas, ascending and descending colon, rectum (also kidneys, outside the digestive system).

? [6] Compare the digestion and absorption of short-chain and long-chain fatty acids.
= Both are freed by lipases. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids are water-soluble enough to diffuse into enterocytes and directly into blood capillaries (hepatic portal vein) without micelles. Long-chain fatty acids need bile-salt micelles, are reassembled into triglycerides, packaged with proteins into chylomicrons and enter lacteals (lymph), reaching blood through the thoracic duct.

? [3] Cassie is a diabetic who drinks heavily. Name the two main types of gallstones and one risk factor for each. Why does fasting raise gallstone risk?
= Cholesterol stones (obesity, diabetes, female sex, rapid weight loss); pigment stones (cirrhosis, alcoholic liver disease, hemolysis). Fasting means little CCK, so the gallbladder rarely empties; bile stagnates and concentrates until crystals form.

? [3] Compare a gallstone blocking the common bile duct with one blocking the main pancreatic duct. Which symptom occurs in both? Which occur only with the pancreatic duct blocked?
= Both: fat maldigestion (steatorrhea). Pancreatic duct only: protein maldigestion and lower duodenal pH (no bicarbonate); pancreatitis. Bile duct only: jaundice and pale stools.

? [3] After cholecystectomy, why should a patient limit dietary fat? Which vitamins may be poorly absorbed? Why can the patient still live without the gallbladder?
= Bile now trickles into the duodenum constantly instead of being released in a burst with a fatty meal, so large fat loads are poorly digested (possible diarrhea). Vitamins A, D, E, K. The liver still makes bile, which flows through the bile ducts.

? [3] Enterogastric reflex: what stimulates it, what does CCK target, and why is the reflex needed?
= Acidic, fatty or hypertonic chyme stretching the duodenum. CCK targets the gallbladder (contract), pancreas (enzymes) and stomach (slows emptying). It prevents the duodenum from being overloaded so it can neutralize acid and finish digestion.

? [2] What is the hepatic portal system, and what is its benefit?
= Veins from the GI tract and spleen drain through the hepatic portal vein to the liver before reaching the heart. The liver processes and stores nutrients and removes toxins, drugs and bacteria first.

? [3] After his stomach is removed, Canaan develops anemia. Explain why. Should he take vitamin B12 orally or by injection?
= No parietal cells means no intrinsic factor, so B12 cannot be absorbed in the ileum, causing pernicious (megaloblastic) anemia; less acid also reduces iron absorption. B12 injections (oral B12 still needs intrinsic factor).

? [4] Name three ions lost in severe diarrhea. Why might the patient hyperventilate? Why does plain water not rehydrate well, and what should she drink?
= Any three of Na+, K+, Cl−, HCO3−. Losing bicarbonate causes metabolic acidosis, so she breathes faster to blow off CO2. Plain water does not replace electrolytes and can dilute blood sodium. An oral rehydration solution with sodium and glucose (absorbed together by SGLT1).

? [3 | TB] Eating a fatty meal before drinking alcohol slows intoxication. Explain using where alcohol is absorbed.
= Some alcohol is absorbed in the stomach, but most is absorbed rapidly in the small intestine. Fat triggers CCK and the enterogastric reflex, slowing gastric emptying, so alcohol stays longer in the stomach where absorption is slow and gastric enzymes break some down.

? [3] Describe what you would see on a liver slide for cirrhosis, fatty liver and hepatitis.
= Cirrhosis: bands of fibrous scar around nodules of liver cells. Fatty liver: hepatocytes filled with round, clear lipid vacuoles. Hepatitis: clusters of lymphocytes around portal areas (inflammation) and damaged hepatocytes.

? [2] If a person swallows while standing on their head, where does the food go? Explain.
= Into the stomach. Swallowing and peristalsis are muscular and work against gravity.

? [4] Jackson, 60, a peanut farmer with heavy drinking and type 2 diabetes, dies of liver failure with jaundice, bruising and fluid in the abdomen. What happened to his liver, what cancer may have developed, and what were his risk factors?
= Cirrhosis progressing to hepatocellular carcinoma. Risk factors: heavy alcohol use, aflatoxin from moldy peanuts, type 2 diabetes (fatty liver). Bruising: low clotting factors; ascites: portal hypertension and low albumin.

? [5] A 54-year-old traveler has fever, nausea, diarrhea and jaundice after eating local shellfish. Name the organ affected and the most likely disease. Which two liver cell types does the virus affect? Why is the disease underreported? Name two ways to prevent it.
= Liver; hepatitis A. Hepatocytes and Kupffer cells. Many cases are mild or have no symptoms. Prevention: hepatitis A vaccine, hand washing, avoiding raw shellfish and untreated water.

? [3] A 19-year-old student develops flu-like illness and yellow eyes weeks after swimming in a lagoon where clothes were washed. Name the disease, explain the yellow eyes, and name one thing she did that could have harmed her liver further.
= Hepatitis A. Damaged hepatocytes cannot process and excrete bilirubin, which builds up in tissues (jaundice). She treated her fever with acetaminophen, which is toxic to the liver at high doses. There is no specific antiviral; treatment is supportive.

? [3] A 66-year-old ex-smoker who drinks daily has weight loss, deep epigastric pain radiating to the back that eases when he leans forward, and greasy stools. CT shows a mass in the tail of the pancreas. Name the diagnosis and three findings that point to the pancreas.
= Pancreatic cancer. Pain radiating to the back and eased by leaning forward; steatorrhea (no pancreatic lipase); epigastric tenderness; smoking and alcohol as risk factors; imaging. Negative Murphy's sign and normal liver and spleen argue against gallbladder disease.

? [2] How would the presentation above differ if the tumor were in the head of the pancreas?
= It could block the common bile duct, causing painless jaundice, dark urine, pale stools and a palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier's sign).

? [3] A newborn in Finland has diarrhea after breastfeeding that stops on soy formula. Name the condition. How does it differ from a milk allergy?
= Congenital lactase deficiency (primary congenital lactose intolerance). An intolerance is a lack of an enzyme, not an immune reaction; a milk allergy is an immune (often IgE) response to milk proteins.

? [3] Name two signs a doctor checks for appendicitis and the surgery used to treat it. What happens if the appendix ruptures?
= McBurney's point tenderness, Rovsing's sign, psoas sign, obturator sign. Appendectomy. Rupture spreads bacteria into the abdomen, causing peritonitis.

? [3] A patient has peritonitis after surgery and stays sick despite strong antibiotics. Why would the doctor insert a drainage tube?
= Pus and infected fluid collect in the peritoneal cavity (an abscess). Antibiotics cannot penetrate an abscess well, so it must be drained.

? [3] Explain how obesity can harm the digestive system. Give three mechanisms.
= Abdominal pressure weakens the lower esophageal sphincter (GERD, hiatal hernia); fatty liver disease; gallstones; visceral fat releases inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α); higher risk of colorectal, esophageal and pancreatic cancer.

? [3] What is Barrett's esophagus, what causes it and why is it important?
= Replacement of the esophagus's stratified squamous lining with simple columnar (intestine-like) cells, caused by long-term acid reflux (GERD). It is a precancerous change that raises the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma.

? [2] Why is lactose intolerance not an allergy? What happens to the undigested lactose?
= It is a lack of lactase, not an immune response. Lactose reaches the colon, draws water in (osmotic diarrhea) and is fermented by bacteria, producing gas and bloating.

? [3] Describe the location of the esophagus from beginning to end.
= It starts behind the cricoid cartilage (about C6), runs through the posterior mediastinum behind the trachea, passes through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm and runs a few centimeters in the abdomen before joining the stomach at the cardia.

? [3] A patient's stomach acid production is high and he has multiple duodenal ulcers. Tests show a gastrin-secreting tumor. Name the syndrome. Why are ulcers forming in the duodenum?
= Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Excess gastrin drives massive HCl secretion that overwhelms the duodenum's bicarbonate buffering.


? [4] Describe protein digestion from mouth to absorption.
= No protein digestion in the mouth. Stomach: HCl denatures proteins and activates pepsin, which cuts them into polypeptides. Small intestine: pancreatic trypsin, chymotrypsin and carboxypeptidase cut polypeptides into smaller peptides; brush-border aminopeptidases and dipeptidases make amino acids, dipeptides and tripeptides. These are absorbed by sodium-dependent cotransport into enterocytes and enter blood capillaries to the hepatic portal vein.

? [4] Describe fat digestion and absorption from mouth to lymph.
= Lingual and gastric lipases start digestion. In the duodenum, CCK releases bile; bile salts emulsify fat. Pancreatic lipase (with colipase) splits triglycerides into fatty acids and monoglycerides, which form micelles with bile salts. They diffuse into enterocytes, are rebuilt into triglycerides, packed into chylomicrons and enter lacteals, then lymph and the thoracic duct.

? [4] Describe how exercise affects the digestive system in the short term and long term.
= Short term: sympathetic activity reduces gut blood flow and slows digestion and gastric emptying; intense exercise can cause nausea, cramps, reflux or diarrhea. Long term: regular moderate exercise speeds colon transit (less constipation), helps control weight, improves insulin sensitivity, lowers risk of colon cancer, gallstones, fatty liver and diverticular disease, and supports a healthy gut microbiome.

? [5] Describe how obesity affects the digestive system. Name at least four conditions linked to it.
= Higher abdominal pressure and a weaker lower esophageal sphincter cause GERD and hiatal hernia; excess fat in the liver causes fatty liver disease, which can progress to cirrhosis; cholesterol-rich bile causes gallstones; inflammatory cytokines from visceral fat damage tissues; higher risk of colorectal, esophageal adenocarcinoma, pancreatic, liver and gallbladder cancers; altered gut microbiome.

? [5] Describe a peptic ulcer at each level: cell, tissue, organ and whole person.
= Cell: H. pylori or NSAIDs damage epithelial cells and reduce mucus and bicarbonate; acid and pepsin digest exposed cells. Tissue: an erosion deepens through the mucosa into the submucosa or muscle layer. Organ: the stomach or duodenal wall can bleed or perforate. Whole person: burning epigastric pain, nausea, black stools, anemia; treated with PPIs, antibiotics for H. pylori and stopping NSAIDs.

? [5] Describe colorectal cancer at each level: cell, tissue, organ and whole person. How is it screened for and prevented?
= Cell: mutations (often APC, then KRAS and p53) let colon epithelial cells divide uncontrollably. Tissue: an adenomatous polyp grows and becomes an invasive adenocarcinoma. Organ: the tumor narrows the colon, bleeds and can spread to lymph nodes and liver. Whole person: blood in stool, change in bowel habits, anemia, weight loss. Screening: colonoscopy (removing polyps), stool tests. Prevention: fiber-rich diet, less red and processed meat, exercise, no smoking, limited alcohol.

? [4] Describe lactose intolerance at the cell, organ and whole-person level.
= Cell: enterocytes in the small intestine make too little lactase on their brush border. Organ: undigested lactose stays in the lumen, drawing in water (osmotic diarrhea); colon bacteria ferment it, producing gas and acids. Whole person: bloating, cramps, gas and diarrhea 30 minutes to 2 hours after dairy; diagnosed with a hydrogen breath test; managed with lactose-free foods or lactase pills.

? [4] Describe hepatitis at the cell, organ and whole-person level.
= Cell: virus (or alcohol, drugs) infects or injures hepatocytes; immune cells kill infected hepatocytes, releasing ALT and AST. Organ: liver inflammation; chronic hepatitis B or C causes fibrosis, cirrhosis and liver cancer. Whole person: fatigue, nausea, jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, right upper abdominal pain; prevented by vaccines (A, B), safe food and water, and avoiding shared needles.

? [4] Describe appendicitis from cause to treatment.
= A fecalith or swollen lymphoid tissue blocks the appendix. Mucus and bacteria build up, pressure rises, blood flow falls and the wall becomes inflamed and can die (gangrene) and rupture. Pain starts near the navel (visceral) and moves to the right lower quadrant (McBurney's point) as the peritoneum is irritated; fever, nausea and high white count follow. Treatment: appendectomy (or antibiotics in selected cases); rupture causes peritonitis.

? [4] Describe diarrhea at the cell and whole-person level, and compare osmotic, secretory and exudative types.
= Cells either absorb too little water (osmotic: unabsorbed solutes such as lactose), secrete too much Cl− and water (secretory: cholera, E. coli toxins) or are damaged and leak blood and pus (exudative: Shigella, ulcerative colitis). Whole person: frequent loose stools, dehydration, loss of Na+, K+ and HCO3−, metabolic acidosis; treated with oral rehydration solution and by treating the cause.
